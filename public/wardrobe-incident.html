The Great Wardrobe Incident was a hide-and-seek-related mishap of contested severity that is, by informal consensus, credited as the founding event of the Department of Hide & Seek. No government body has formally acknowledged that the Incident occurred, that it involved a wardrobe, or that anyone was, in the technical sense, incidented. This article proceeds on the assumption that all three are true.

Background

Prior to the Incident, games of hide and seek were conducted on what historians now refer to as the "honour system" — an informal arrangement in which participants located one another without the benefit of a Hiding Declaration, a Freedom of Information response, or an appointed Tribunal.[2] Proponents of this earlier system describe it, somewhat wistfully, as "fun." The Department disputes this characterisation.

The Incident

Accounts vary, but converge on the following: during a routine round of hide and seek, one participant (name withheld, hereafter "the Hider") concealed themselves inside a bedroom wardrobe and was, for a duration nobody present has ever agreed upon, not found. Search parties combed the residence. Furniture was moved. At least one search party reportedly checked the same laundry basket four times.[3]

Persistent rumours that the wardrobe in question opened onto somewhere else entirely have never been substantiated, and the Department declines to comment further, citing an ongoing internal review[1] and a separate, unrelated fondness for coats.

The Hider eventually emerged of their own accord, apparently unaware that anything was amiss, and asked what was for dinner. This is, depending on who is telling the story, either the funniest or the most infuriating detail of the entire affair.

Aftermath

In the weeks that followed, a subcommittee was convened to ensure that no hider could again go unaccounted-for without at least some paperwork to show for it. This subcommittee drafted what would become Form 27B, the Hiding Declaration now required of all participants before a round may commence, and proposed the Freedom of Information response now issued to seekers in place of a plain list of names.[4]

The subcommittee was made permanent later that year under the name it retains today: the Department of Hide & Seek, a Sub-Committee of the Ministry of Games, Pastimes & Related Paperwork.

Legacy

The Incident is now cited, with varying degrees of seriousness, as the origin of most modern hide-and-seek regulation: the Tribunal of Peers convened to hear contested findings, the practice of pausing official proceedings for the duration of a dispute, and the wardrobe-shaped blind spot in Departmental risk assessments that nobody has yet had the courage to close.

A minority view holds that the Incident never happened at all, and that the Department simply preferred having an origin story to admitting it enjoys the paperwork for its own sake. The Department declines to comment on this too.
